Lesson Overview

What You Are Learning

Learning Targets

  • Observe the rocket as a complete assembly under real flight loading.
  • Evaluate whether the rocket remained stable and intact.
  • Inspect the rocket after landing for assembly or manufacturing failures.
  • Use flight evidence to identify one future design improvement.

Unit 3 Progression

The unit ends with the assembly doing what it was designed to do: leave the launch tube as a complete physical system and provide evidence for the next iteration.

Compressed-Air Rocket Challenge

Project Requirements

These requirements apply throughout Lessons 3.6–3.14.

Launcher Interface

1/2 in PVC Reference Tube

  • The launcher tube outside diameter is 0.840 in (21.34 mm).
  • Model the launch tube as a separate grounded Fusion reference component.
  • You choose the rocket's internal launch clearance, but the finished rocket must slide freely over the actual PVC tube without binding.
  • Before choosing the rocket's internal bore dimension, measure the actual unpressurized classroom launch tube. The 0.840 in (21.34 mm) diameter is the nominal outside-diameter reference, not the target bore size.
  • The launcher itself is teacher equipment and is not modified as part of this project.
Printed Structure

Modular 3D-Printed Rocket

  • The structural rocket body is 3D printed in classroom-approved filament.
  • Use at least 3 major printed modules: a lower launch/fin module, a main body module, and a nose/upper module.
  • The rocket must have at least 2 removable printed-to-printed interfaces.
  • Major printed modules must be disassembled without damaging the parts; permanent adhesive is not allowed between major modules.
Student Choice

Connection Method

  • You choose how major modules connect.
  • Examples include keyed/slip couplers with retention, twist-lock/bayonet features, screws/inserts, snap-fit features, or another approved method.
  • The connection must keep the rocket aligned and secure during handling and flight-readiness checks.
  • If a connection uses hardware or a tool, it must still be serviceable without damaging the printed parts.
Fins

Laser-Cut Fin Assembly

  • Use 3 or 4 identical fins, equally spaced at 120° or 90°.
  • Fins are laser-cut from 1/8 in acrylic or plywood.
  • Use 3.175 mm as the nominal CAD thickness, then measure the actual stock and use that measurement for the final fin interface.
  • Fins must use a repeatable locating feature such as slots, tabs, keyed pockets, or another approved feature. Surface gluing alone is not an acceptable locating method.
  • Adhesive may be used to retain the fins after the fit/alignment check is approved.
Material Efficiency

Lightweight by Design

  • Primary body walls should generally be 1.2–2.0 mm thick; 1.6 mm is the recommended target.
  • Geometry thicker than 2.5 mm should be limited to local interfaces, couplers, fin mounts, fastener features, or other justified reinforcement.
  • Total predicted 3D-printed material should be 100 g or less.
  • The finished rocket should have a total mass of 125 g or less before the flight-readiness review.
Manufacturing & Safety

Build for the Classroom Process

  • Every printed component must fit the classroom printer build volume and be designed to print without support material.
  • No support material may be required inside the launch bore or any mating interface.
  • The nose must be rounded/blunt, and exposed fin corners/edges must be made safe before flight.
  • Only the instructor operates, pressurizes, or changes settings on the compressed-air launcher.
Launch Safety

Instructor-controlled compressed-air test

The instructor operates and pressurizes the PVC launcher using established class procedures and fixed test settings. Students remain in the designated observation area and do not alter the launcher or pressure system.

Assignment

Flight-Test and Inspect the Final Rocket

The flight test is a qualification event, not a maximum-distance contest. The goal is to determine whether the modular assembly performs safely and remains intact.

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Complete the final preflight confirmation: Verify the Lesson 3.13 approval is still valid and no part has changed since inspection.
  2. Prepare the observation record: Be ready to record launch release, flight stability, assembly integrity, and landing condition.
  3. Turn the rocket over for launch: The instructor installs and launches the rocket using the established compressed-air apparatus and fixed class test settings.
  4. Observe launch release: Record whether the rocket leaves the PVC tube cleanly without binding or shedding parts.
  5. Observe flight behavior: Record whether the flight is generally stable or shows immediate tumble, severe yaw, or another obvious instability.
  6. Observe assembly integrity: Record whether major modules and fins remain attached throughout flight and landing.
  7. Recover and inspect: After the instructor indicates the area is safe, inspect the rocket for cracks, loosened interfaces, fin damage, or deformation.
  8. Disassemble the rocket: Verify the serviceable modules can still be separated after the test.
  9. Compare with the CAD: Identify whether any post-flight issue originated from geometry, interface design, material use, or manufacturing.
  10. Write one next-iteration recommendation: State the single most important change you would make before a second design cycle.

Before You Submit

  • The rocket had instructor flight approval.
  • Flight observations are recorded rather than guessed afterward.
  • Post-flight damage is documented before repair.
  • Assembly integrity and serviceability are evaluated.
  • The final recommendation is tied to actual flight evidence.
Submission

What to Submit

Submit only the following assignment-specific deliverables in Google Classroom:

  • Flight-test and post-flight report (one PDF): LastName_FirstName_3_14_FlightReport.pdf recording launch release (clean/binding), observed flight stability, whether fins and modules remained attached, landing condition, and any observed failure. Do not guess missing observations.
  • Post-flight photos (inside the PDF): overall rocket after recovery plus close-ups of any damage or loosened interfaces; note 'no visible damage' if appropriate.
  • Engineering conclusion (in the PDF): write one evidence-based design improvement, referencing an observed flight or post-flight result. A successful launch is not required for a strong report.

Grading: Engineering documentation and evidence-based analysis matter more than maximum flight distance.
