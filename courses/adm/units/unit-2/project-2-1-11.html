AI-Powered Sorting Machine

Engineer a custom automated sorting machine that recognizes parts with AI vision and physically routes them into the correct collection bins.

Engineering Design Challenge

Your Mission

Working in a team of 2–3 students, design, fabricate, program, and test a standalone, AI-powered sorting machine that uses a trained model to identify parts and automatically move them to the correct destinations. Choose either LEGO components or nuts and bolts. Each team defines three visually distinguishable sorting classes and builds three product bins plus a fourth reject bin for unknown or uncertain items.

100 points60 engineering + 40 performanceManual loading allowedFully custom construction

Option A: LEGO Components

Choose three different part types or shapes, such as 2×2 bricks, 2×4 bricks, and plates. The classifier must recognize part identity or shape; simply sorting by color is insufficient.

Option B: Nuts & Bolts

Choose three distinct hardware categories—for example, nuts, short bolts, and longer bolts. Use clean, instructor-approved, blunt hardware. Your camera and feeder must handle glare and variable object positions.

Preparation: Complete ADM 2.1.8–2.1.10 in Programming Studio before this final project. Those guided lessons prepare the sensor, vision and actuator skills; the final project still requires your own trained classifier, design and supervised physical validation.

Required equipment and programming

  • Camera: external USB webcam (available from the instructor).
  • Computer: classroom Windows computer running Python with OpenCV through a browser-based interface. Locally hosted JupyterLab is the recommended environment.
  • Vision and programming: Each team is responsible for researching, training, testing, and explaining its machine-learning classifier and the Python/OpenCV and Arduino programs. AI coding assistance is permitted for generating, improving, explaining, and debugging code. Students must review, test, adapt, and understand all code they use. The instructor does not provide a completed starter program or finished sorting solution.
  • Required hardware controller: every team must use the instructor-provided Arduino to operate its sorting mechanism. A starter servo is provided; teams may propose additional approved motors, sensors, and electronics. Python communicates classification decisions to the Arduino, which controls the actuators.
  • Fabrication: student-selected, teacher-approved methods (3D printing, laser cutting, hand tools, or other techniques). No fixed additional materials budget, but all purchases require approval.
How it works: USB webcam → Python/OpenCV image capture → trained model class prediction → Python serial command → Arduino → servo/motor-driven sorter → three class bins or reject bin.
First approval gate: Before fabricating the powered system, demonstrate live webcam capture from Python/OpenCV and successful Python-to-Arduino communication on the actual Windows computer. A cloud-hosted notebook is acceptable only if the instructor verifies that it can reliably connect to both devices. The Arduino must be programmed separately to accept and validate the commands it receives.
Project programming expectations

Using AI to Help Write Code

You may use AI tools to help write and improve code for this project. AI can assist with Python/OpenCV image capture, machine-learning model training and inference, Arduino programming, serial communication, actuator control, debugging, and explaining error messages.

Permitted AI assistance

  • Generate or revise Python and Arduino code, including functions and example implementations.
  • Explain code, libraries, algorithms, or errors and suggest debugging steps.
  • Help plan software structure, analyze test failures, or improve reliability.
  • Suggest alternative approaches when your first implementation does not work.

Your responsibility

  • Understand and be able to explain the code that your team submits and demonstrates.
  • Test AI-generated suggestions with your actual USB webcam, Python/OpenCV environment, and Arduino; correct anything that does not work.
  • Make your own engineering decisions about the classes, training data, mechanism, safety, and revisions.
  • Briefly document which AI tools assisted with your code and what you changed or verified.
  • Report real model performance and physical trial results. Never invent training images, validation results, or successful sorting trials.
How this affects grading: Using AI to help code does not reduce your grade. Your grade depends on your documented engineering work, your ability to explain and troubleshoot the programs, and the performance of the sorting device. A copied or AI-generated program that your team cannot explain, test, or safely operate does not meet the project requirements.
Minimum Requirements

Mechanical, AI, and Testing Specifications

Custom machine

  • Three student-selected part classes, three collection bins, and one unknown/reject bin.
  • Custom frame, object presentation area, fixed webcam mount, powered sorting mechanism, and secure collection bins.
  • Manual loading of one object at a time is allowed. Classification and routing after insertion must be automatic.
  • Servo gates, rotating chutes, pushers, and other suitable mechanisms are permitted.
  • Automatic hopper feeding, additional categories, or higher sorting speed are optional extensions without bonus points.

AI training

  • Collect 50 labeled training images for each of three classes: 150 training images total.
  • Keep 15 separate, previously unseen validation images for each class: 45 validation images total.
  • Submit the student-trained model or reproducible training procedure, sample predictions, and overall validation accuracy.
  • Use actual model predictions to drive the sorter. OpenCV can capture, crop, and preprocess images, but color thresholds, fixed timers, or hand-picked classes alone do not qualify.
  • Uncertain or unfamiliar parts must route to reject or trigger an approved safe handling procedure.
Final demonstration: 30 randomized trials (10 from each known class) plus 5 separate unknown/reject trials—35 trials altogether. The target is 80% known-part accuracy (at least 24/30 correct), with advanced recognition for 28–30 correct. Track throughput, resets, jams, and one evidence-based improvement. The instructor chooses the random presentation order.

Safety

Use approved low-voltage power supplies and secure wiring. Verify the Arduino-to-servo electrical design before activation: a servo may need its own properly rated power source and a shared ground. Guard pinch points, keep hands clear when powered, wear safety glasses while fabricating, and stop if parts jam. Do not use unapproved mains wiring, sharp or hazardous objects, or unsupervised powered testing.

What counts as success?

The machine recognizes a previously unseen physical object using the trained classifier, automatically commands the Arduino, and routes the object to the correct bin. A screen-only demonstration, manually controlled servo, or fixed sorting sequence is not an integrated AI sorting machine.

Extended engineering project · Approximately 40 × 45-minute classes

Project Timeline & Graded Milestones

Plan for approximately 40 class periods (30 instructional hours) for independent AI development, custom fabrication, Arduino integration and iterative testing. The proposed project window is November 11, 2026 through January 28, 2027, excluding November 23–27, winter break and January 18. Teams can work on multiple parts of the project in parallel, but must receive approval before moving past safety-critical checkpoints.

Phase 1 · 5 classes · Nov 11–17

Research, environment setup & system proposal. Choose LEGO or hardware and three distinct sorting categories; investigate Python/OpenCV and independently research an AI method. Confirm USB webcam capture and Python-to-Arduino serial communication, draw two viable concepts and assign team responsibilities.

Checkpoint 1 · Nov 17 · 10 points: Feasibility and design proposal.

Phase 2 · 8 classes · Nov 18–Dec 4

Training data & AI development. Gather 150 training images and 45 separate validation images, independently train a classifier, verify predictions and calculate accuracy. Refine lighting, camera placement and decision/reject rules. Sketch preliminary machine layouts in parallel.

Checkpoint 2 · Dec 4 · 15 points: Trained classification model and evidence.

Phase 3 · 7 classes · Dec 7–15

Mechanical design & manufacturing preparation. Create assembly CAD or dimensioned drawings of the feeder, camera mount, sorter and four bins. Select materials, prepare fabrication files, evaluate required servo motion and create a safe wiring/power plan. Begin approved fabrication when ready.

Intermediate progress review: Manufacturing-ready design and approved bill of materials; incorporated into Checkpoint 3.

Phase 4 · 10 classes · Dec 16–Jan 13

Fabrication, assembly & mechanism iteration. Build, fit and revise parts; secure the webcam and sorting components; wire Arduino and drive the actuator under controlled tests. Account for printer queues, failed fits and rebuilding. Resume physical testing after winter break.

Checkpoint 3 · Jan 13 · 15 points: Completed safe custom assembly and electronics demonstration.

Phase 5 · 7 classes · Jan 14–25

AI-to-Arduino integration & debugging. Link live OpenCV/model results to Arduino motor/servo commands. Route objects into all three class bins and reject; record misclassifications, delays and jams. Run mixed trial batches, revise the code or mechanism, and retest.

Checkpoint 4 · Jan 25 · 15 points: Live autonomous sorting through every destination.

Phase 6 · 3 classes · Jan 26–28

Test readiness, final demonstration & analysis. Complete preflight checks, document a measured improvement and organize technical files. Demonstrate 30 randomized known parts (10 from each class) plus five separate unknown/reject items, then review accuracy, throughput and reliability.

Checkpoint 5 · Jan 27 · 5 points: Final test readiness.

Final performance · Jan 28 · 40 points: Supervised assessment.

Proposed dates only: This expanded project overlaps the existing January Unit 3 start and follows the three Studio preparation lessons and requires a teacher-approved schedule. Evidence submitted throughout the project

What to Submit

  1. Checkpoint 1 — Proposal: two concepts, chosen class definitions, system architecture, team roles, hardware/material plan, safety plan, and live webcam/Arduino communications evidence.
  2. Checkpoint 2 — AI model: labeled 150-image training set, 45 unseen validation images, model or reproducible training code, sample predictions, and overall validation accuracy.
  3. Checkpoint 3 — Build: CAD or fully dimensioned technical drawings, fabrication files, parts list, photographs, Arduino wiring, and physical mechanism approval.
  4. Checkpoint 4 — Integration: commented Python/OpenCV and Arduino programs, model files, and physical demonstration of all three routes plus reject.
  5. Checkpoint 5 — Readiness: safety/preflight checklist, debugging records, evidence of one improvement, and organized submission files.
  6. Final assessment: 30 known-object and five unknown-object trial results, calculated known-object accuracy and throughput, failure/reset record, final demo, and short reflection.

Each checkpoint is graded separately. Keep all CAD, training data, model, Python/Arduino code, and build documentation together in a clearly organized team folder. Include a brief note describing any AI tools used to assist with coding and the changes or tests your team performed. Everyone must be able to explain their contribution and code. If a checkpoint is incomplete, revise and request reapproval before moving to the next safety-critical stage.
