01 / What does “better” mean?

Five important material properties

Different materials may perform best for different engineering requirements. A material that is very stiff may not be the lightest or the toughest.

Stiffness

How strongly a material resists elastic deformation. Material stiffness is commonly measured by elastic modulus E. Structural bending stiffness depends on EI, so geometry also matters.

Strength

Resistance to failure or permanent deformation under a specified loading mode. Tensile, compressive and bending strengths are not interchangeable.

Density

Mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V. Low density can help meet a mass limit, but density alone does not establish load capacity.

Ductility

Ability to undergo plastic deformation before fracture. Not every material yields in the same way; brittle materials may fracture with little plastic deformation.

Toughness

Energy absorbed per unit volume before fracture, represented qualitatively by total area under a stress–strain curve.

Directional behavior

Properties may depend on orientation: wood grain, plywood plies and 3D-print layers can all make a material behave differently in different directions.

Material behavior vs. structure behavior

The elastic modulus E describes material stiffness. A beam's bending stiffness EI also includes its area moment of inertia I, which depends on the cross-section shape and orientation.

Critical distinction: A thicker strip can bend less even if made from the same material. Lower deflection alone does not mean the material has a larger E or higher strength.

Stress and strain

σ = F/A  ·  ε = ΔL/L₀  ·  E = σ/ε (linear elastic range)

Axial stress σ is measured in N/mm², strain ε has no units, and E is the initial elastic slope of the stress–strain curve.

1 N/mm² = 1 MPa. Always state the loading mode when comparing material strengths.

02 / Learn to read evidence

What does a stress–strain graph reveal?

The initial slope identifies the elastic modulus. The curve's later behavior helps describe yielding, ductility and fracture—but you must not assume all materials have the same shaped curve.

Understand the slope

In a simple tensile test, stress ÷ strain in the initial linear region gives E. A steeper initial slope means greater material axial stiffness for the specific loading direction being measured.

Why isn't a high E the same as high strength?

Elastic modulus describes how much the material stretches under a stress in the elastic region, not the stress at which it yields, cracks or breaks. Strong and stiff are separate properties.

Why toughness matters

Two materials can reach similar maximum stress yet absorb very different amounts of energy before fracture. One can fail suddenly; another may deform substantially.

Where is toughness on the graph?

It is associated with total area under the stress–strain curve through fracture, expressed as energy per unit volume. The graphic is qualitative and contains no material-specific data.

03 / Natural and manufactured materials

Wood grain, plywood plies and print orientation

Structure inside a material can change its behavior. For wood, grain, moisture, knots and fiber alignment matter; plywood uses bonded cross-oriented veneer layers.

Wood strips and craft sticks

Balsa and basswood strips are natural wood. A property measured along the fibers may differ greatly from a property measured across the fibers. Defects, moisture, species and sample variability complicate simple comparisons.

For bridge members: record grain direction and avoid assuming each wooden strip has identical stiffness or strength.

Plywood and layered fabrication

Plywood bonds multiple veneer plies, usually with successive grain directions crossed. This improves directional balance and dimensional stability, but bending properties still depend on layup, thickness and panel orientation.

For bridge gussets: document thickness, grain/face direction and joint layout. Cutting defects and glue quality affect real performance.

Printed polymer orientation is another useful comparison

A 3D-printed coupon can behave differently when loaded along deposited roads versus across layer bonds. Results depend on the filament, processing temperature, voids, perimeter count and build orientation.

Unit 2 manufacturing rule: PLA/PETG printed parts are permitted only as approved joint fittings, brackets, gussets or mounts. No 3D-printed bridge chords, beams, diagonals or decks. Acrylic is not an approved bridge material. See the Unit 2 construction requirements before planning fabrication.

05 / How engineers test stiffness

Three-point bending: control the specimen dimensions

A rectangular strip supported at both ends is pushed downward at its midpoint. Under simple linear-elastic beam assumptions, smaller center displacement at a fixed load suggests greater flexural stiffness of that specific specimen.

Use the beam equation

I = bt³ / 12
δ = PL³ / (48EI)

For a slender simply supported rectangular beam under a centered force P, I is the second moment of area and δ is predicted center displacement. E is an effective elastic bending modulus for the tested orientation.

What must stay constant?

Use the same span L, specimen width b, specimen thickness t, and applied P to isolate differences due to E. All measurements need units. Repeat tests with multiple samples to account for variability.

Limit: This model assumes small elastic deflection and shear/bending behavior adequately described by simple Euler–Bernoulli beam theory. It does not predict when a material will fracture, delaminate or buckle.

Check Investigation A

Vary the thickness

Keep materials and load unchanged. Increase t from 3 to 6 mm. What happens to I and δ?

Reveal explanation

Since I ∝ t³, doubling thickness multiplies I by 8 and the predicted deflection becomes 1/8 as large at the same E, load, span and width. Specimen material volume and mass double.

Check Investigation B

Vary the span

Keep E, b, t and P unchanged. Increase span L from 100 mm to 200 mm. What happens to the predicted center deflection?

Reveal explanation

δ ∝ L³ for this simply supported center-loaded beam. Doubling the span multiplies predicted center deflection by 8. The physical specimen must still be long enough to reach both supports.

07 / Fully worked example

Two equal-sized coupons, different elastic moduli

Compare illustrative basswood E = 9,000 N/mm² against illustrative plywood E = 6,000 N/mm² using the same 150 mm span, 12 mm width, 3 mm thickness and centered 5 N force.

Step 1 · Cross-section geometry

I = 12(3³)/12 = 27 mm⁴

Both samples use the same I, which isolates the assumed material-property difference.

Step 2 · Specimen A

δA = 5(150³)/[48(9,000)(27)]

δA ≈ 1.447 mm (model result).

Step 3 · Specimen B

δB = 5(150³)/[48(6,000)(27)]

δB ≈ 2.170 mm (model result).

Step 4 · Interpret carefully

A predicts less deflection because its assumed modulus is larger. This says nothing about which sample fails first, which has better joints, or the properties of actual classroom stock.

What about specimen mass?

Test-strip volume = (150)(12)(3) = 5,400 mm³ = 5.40 cm³. With illustrative densities 0.42 g/cm³ and 0.65 g/cm³, A is 2.27 g and B is 3.51 g. These are nominal estimates for this test strip, not full bridge mass or supplier specifications.

Evidence vs. assumptions: Use measured sample mass and load/displacement in Lesson 2.13 to replace these theoretical estimates and compare multiple specimens.

08 / Materials selection matrix

Choose materials for three structural jobs

Evaluate alternatives by function rather than declaring one universally “best.” Consider a shelf in bending, a support post in compression, and a bridge connection/gusset that transfers load.

ApplicationEssential prioritiesOptions to compareQuestions to justify your choice
Shelf / horizontal stripBending stiffness, strength, span, massPlywood panel; basswood strip; balsa stripWhat E and section I resist bending? Is thickness changed? What about fiber orientation?
Support post / truss strutCompression resistance, buckling, straightness, massBasswood; balsa; craft-stick laminateHow long and slender is the member? Which specimen defects or joint misalignment matter?
Bridge gusset / connectionLoad transfer, connection integrity, manufacturability, massPlywood gusset; interlocking wood joint; approved printed fittingIs the connection within the unit's size, material and fastener rules? Can forces transfer without splitting or slip?

A defensible scoring method

Rank each option on a 1–5 scale for stiffness, strength/failure resistance, density/mass efficiency, fabrication/connection fit and rule compliance. Support each score with a measured value, cited reference, or explicitly labeled hypothesis.

Do not award high scores just because a material feels rigid when held in your hand. Use the same geometry and testing conditions.

Unit 2 bridge reminders

Approved primary bridge materials include balsa, basswood, craft sticks and plywood. Local plywood gussets, printed connection fittings and M3 nuts/bolts have specific dimensional/manufacturing limits. Acrylic and printed continuous structural members are prohibited.

Review the Unit 2 specification before proposing a physical construction choice.

09 / Independent checks

Test your understanding before opening each answer

Answer in complete sentences or calculations, using correct units and stating assumptions.

Check A

Strength or stiffness?

Two samples deflect the same amount at 5 N but one breaks at a lower load. Are they equally strong?

Reveal explanation

Not necessarily. Similar displacement at 5 N suggests comparable specimen stiffness under those conditions, but the one that fails earlier has lower observed capacity in that test.

Check B

Density calculation

A specimen has volume 8.0 cm³ and mass 4.0 g. Find density.

Reveal explanation

ρ = m/V = 4.0/8.0 = 0.50 g/cm³.

Check C

Thickness matters

For a constant E and beam width, what happens to bending stiffness EI when rectangular beam thickness doubles?

Reveal explanation

I = bt³/12, so I becomes 8 times larger, and EI becomes eight times larger if E is unchanged.

Check D

Grain orientation

Why should grain orientation be recorded on a basswood sample's test report?

Reveal explanation

Wood is anisotropic/orthotropic; properties along fibers can differ from those across fibers. The observed stiffness/strength can therefore depend on how the specimen was cut and loaded.

Check E

Toughness and stiffness

A material has a steep initial stress–strain slope, but fractures at low strain. Is high stiffness evidence that it is tough?

Reveal explanation

No. Stiffness refers to the initial elastic slope E; toughness relates to total energy absorbed up to fracture. A brittle material can be stiff but not particularly tough.

Check F

Fair comparison

You test a 3 mm plywood strip and a 6 mm basswood strip of equal span. Can the smaller deflection prove the winning material has the higher E?

Reveal explanation

No. Thickness alters I with the cube of thickness. To compare material E, control width, thickness, span and load and use an appropriate material model.

Check G

Mass versus safety

Why might the lightest material be a poor choice for a long compression member?

Reveal explanation

It could lack sufficient stiffness, column stability or compressive strength. Slender members may buckle, while connections and fabrication defects can limit real capacity.

Check H

Printed parts

Can you design the entire bridge chord as a 3D-printed PLA beam because PLA coupons seem stiff?

Reveal explanation

No. The Unit 2 manufacturing rules permit approved printed connectors/fittings only, not fully printed chords or beams. A coupon stiffness comparison does not override the assignment constraints.

Student submission

Exactly what to submit

Complete the POE 2.11 Structural Materials & Properties comparison worksheet or a teacher-approved engineering notebook equivalent. The main deliverable is a materials selection matrix supported by material-property evidence.

Required evidence

  • A five-property vocabulary comparison distinguishing stiffness, strength, density, ductility and toughness.
  • One annotated sketch showing grain alignment or plywood cross-oriented plies and its relevance to the loading direction.
  • A fully labeled two-material bending comparison with sample dimensions, force and assumed/recorded E values.
  • Predicted deflections (or teacher-approved test observations), estimated/measured masses and units.
  • A materials selection matrix evaluating at least three candidate materials across shelf, post and gusset applications.
  • Justification for your choices with at least two specific quantitative observations and one limitation/uncertainty.

Quality check

  • Do not label hypothetical E or density numbers as real measured properties.
  • State all controlled variables for the material comparison.
  • Use mm for specimen dimensions, N for load, N/mm² for E and mm for deflection.
  • Keep claims about strength and failure separate from elastic bending predictions.
  • Ensure bridge-related choices follow the approved construction rules.
  • Include any additional assigned worksheet or homework questions.
