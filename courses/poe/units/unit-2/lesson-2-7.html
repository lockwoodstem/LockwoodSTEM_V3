Cross-Sections, Centroids & Structural Stiffness

Engineering question: Why can two spars made from the same amount of material bend very different amounts?

Coming In

What You Already Know

You now understand load paths and bracing. The next design lever is the cross-sectional shape of the members carrying bending loads.

Learning Targets

By the End of This Lesson

  • Explain why material location affects bending stiffness.
  • Identify the centroid and neutral-axis idea qualitatively.
  • Compare spar cross-sections for stiffness and mass efficiency.
Design Principle

Move material away from the neutral axis

You do not need advanced beam equations to use this idea. For the same material and mass, a deeper section generally resists bending better than a shallow one because more material is placed farther from the neutral axis.

Learn the Concept

What You Need to Know

These notes are intended to be complete enough to learn the core idea directly from the page.

Centroid

The centroid is the geometric center of an area. For a uniform cross-section, it helps locate the neutral axis used in bending analysis.

Neutral axis

During bending, one side of a member is in tension and the opposite side is in compression. Between them is a region with very little longitudinal strain: the neutral axis.

Material placement

Material farther from the neutral axis contributes much more to bending stiffness than material concentrated near the center. That is why I-beams and box sections can be efficient.

Second moment of area

The second moment of area, often called area moment of inertia, describes how a cross-section's area is distributed relative to an axis. A larger value generally means less bending for the same material and load.

Wing spar implication

A flat strip, vertical web, I-section, and box spar can use similar amounts of material but produce very different deflection.

Worked comparison

Imagine a thin rectangular strip used as a spar. If the strip is laid flat, its vertical depth is small and it bends easily. Rotate the same strip 90° so its larger dimension is vertical and the bending stiffness increases dramatically even though the mass is unchanged.

Apply It to Your Wing

Turn the Concept into Engineering Evidence

  1. Compare at least four spar cross-sections such as flat strip, vertical strip, I-section, channel, or box section.
  2. For each, predict stiffness, mass efficiency, and likely failure mode.
  3. If materials are available, perform a quick equal-load deflection comparison.
  4. Select one or two promising spar geometries for future wing concepts.

Spar geometry investigation

Self-Check

Can You Explain It?

1. Why can rotating a rectangular strip make it much stiffer without changing mass?

The rotation increases the section depth and moves material farther from the neutral axis, increasing resistance to bending.

2. Does the strongest material always make the best spar?

No. Stiffness, density, geometry, manufacturability, joints, and the overall mass limit also matter.
