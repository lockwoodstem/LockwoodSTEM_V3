Cross-Sections, Centroids & Member Stiffness

Engineering question: Why can two bridge members made from the same amount of material bend very different amounts?

Learn the Concept

What You Need to Know

Centroid

The centroid is the geometric center of an area. For a uniform section, it helps locate the neutral axis used in bending analysis.

Neutral axis

During bending, one side is stretched and the opposite side is compressed. Between them is a region with very little longitudinal strain.

Material placement

Material farther from the neutral axis contributes much more to bending stiffness than material concentrated near the center.

Second moment of area

Area moment of inertia describes how cross-sectional area is distributed about an axis. Larger values generally mean less bending for the same material and loading.

Member implication

A flat strip, vertical strip, I-section, and box member can use similar mass but produce very different deflection.

Simple comparison

Lay a rectangular strip flat and it bends easily. Rotate the same strip so its larger dimension is vertical and it becomes dramatically stiffer without changing mass. Geometry changed; material did not.

Member Stiffness Lab

Measure the Effect of Cross-Section Geometry

  1. Predict the stiffness ranking of the provided member samples or orientations.
  2. Use the same material, cantilever span, test load, load position, and measurement method whenever possible.
  3. Measure tip deflection for each geometry and repeat the measurement for consistency.
  4. Compare flat, edge-oriented, built-up/I-section, and box/closed-section behavior when samples are available.
  5. Use measured evidence to select a preliminary member geometry for the bridge.

Self-Check

Can You Explain It?

1. Why can rotating a rectangular strip increase stiffness without changing mass?

It increases the section depth and moves material farther from the neutral axis.

2. Does the strongest material automatically make the best member?

No. Stiffness, density, geometry, manufacturability, joining, and mass all matter.
