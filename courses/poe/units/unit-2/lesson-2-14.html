Structural Efficiency

Engineering question: How can we make a structure lighter while still meeting every strength, stiffness and manufacturing requirement?

Analyze · Compare · Optimize

Mass matters—but so do the requirements.

Calculate load-per-mass and stiffness-per-mass ratios, then apply qualification limits to proposed bridge designs.

Materials: your six-specimen 2.13 testing report, calculator, engineering notebook and mass estimates or actual measurements.

Learning targets

Use evidence to make a lightweight design decision

1 · Measure efficiency

Calculate a performance-to-mass ratio with the correct units and understand what each ratio proves.

2 · Apply experimental results

Use actual stiffness and mass data from the six different 2.13 specimens without claiming the experiment measured breaking strength.

3 · Budget and qualify

Plan complete bridge mass and reject numerically noncompliant concepts before comparing them.

Suggested pacing: 10 minutes for key concepts, 10 minutes for videos, 15 minutes for material analysis and 15 minutes for bridge comparison and mass budgeting. Finish independent practice as assigned.

01 / Understand structural efficiency

Don't remove material without understanding its job

Engineers search for ways to direct forces through efficiently placed members. Reducing mass can improve a design—unless the changes introduce excessive deformation, buckling or joint failure.

A full structure

Supported load per finished mass

ηload = supported load (N) / completed mass (g)

Units: N/g. Specify whether the numerator is a qualification load or an experimentally demonstrated higher load. The same qualification load divided by bridge mass measures mass efficiency at that requirement, not ultimate failure capacity.

A tested strip

Bending stiffness per specimen mass

ηstrip = k (N/mm) / specimen mass (g)

Units: N/(mm·g). The value k is the measured early linear force–deflection slope from 2.13. It describes the specimen in that test configuration. It is not ultimate strength.

The 2.13 lab is non-destructive. Students did not establish ultimate failure force for the steel, carbon-fiber, wood, aluminum, polyethylene or Delrin strips, and cannot calculate a verified “breaking strength-to-weight” ratio from those data.

03 / Follow the worked examples

Calculate first; interpret the result second

Example A · Bridge load per gram

Hypothetical bridge A supports 120 N with a mass of 100 g. Bridge B supports 150 N with a mass of 140 g.

A: 120/100 = 1.20 N/g
B: 150/140 ≈ 1.07 N/g

A has higher N/g, while B supports a larger total load. Do not choose until you check deflection, width, span and material rules. These are instructional values, not actual classroom bridge results.

Example B · Test specimen stiffness per gram

In a hypothetical comparison, specimen A has k = 8 N/mm and mass 12 g; specimen B has k = 11 N/mm and mass 20 g.

A: 8/12 = 0.667 N/(mm·g)
B: 11/20 = 0.550 N/(mm·g)

B has greater total specimen stiffness, but A has a better stiffness-per-mass ratio. Cross-section and grain/fiber orientation may differ, so this is not a pure comparison of intrinsic material properties.

Example C · Predict mass from density

An illustrative wood member occupies 36,000 mm³ and has assumed density 0.50 g/cm³.

36,000 mm³ ÷ 1,000 = 36 cm³
m = ρV = (0.50)(36) = 18 g

This estimates the member alone; a finished structure includes adhesives, gussets, fasteners, braces and approved fittings. Replace assumed densities with actual measurements whenever possible.

08 / Check your understanding

Independent practice with explanations

1 · Load per gram

A structure supports 160 N and weighs 125 g. Find supported load-to-mass ratio.

Reveal worked explanation

160/125 = 1.28 N/g. Explain which load was tested and check every other qualification criterion.

2 · Density to mass

A wood component occupies 24,000 mm³, with assumed density 0.55 g/cm³. Estimate its mass.

Reveal worked explanation

24,000 mm³ = 24 cm³; mass = 24 × 0.55 = 13.2 g.

3 · Mandatory deflection

Bridge X weighs 80 g but deflects 13 mm at 100 N. Bridge Y weighs 110 g and deflects 8 mm. Which meets the deflection limit?

Reveal worked explanation

Y passes the ≤10 mm deflection test. X fails regardless of its higher raw N/g ratio.

4 · Missing steel data

You cannot measure steel deflection before reaching your force gauge's safe capacity. What k/m should you enter?

Reveal worked explanation

None. Record “not measurable with the available gauge” instead of putting zero or an unmeasured published value.

5 · Paint stick geometry

Why can't raw bending k/m values alone compare the intrinsic properties of a 7 mm-thick paint stick and 3.175 mm-thick bars?

Reveal worked explanation

Beam bending I scales with thickness cubed. The different sections and grain/fiber orientations can drive the result.

6 · Full mass budget

Your wood/plywood mass is 134 g and connections/additives add 24 g. Does the structure satisfy 150 g?

Reveal worked explanation

Total = 158 g, which is 8 g over the limit. Redesign before fabrication.

7 · Stiffness versus failure force

Does the greatest specimen bending slope prove the greatest breaking strength?

Reveal worked explanation

No. Non-destructive bending slope measures elastic stiffness. Strength, toughness and failure capacity require other approved tests.

8 · Why keep bracing?

Why might removing a 5 g diagonal decrease structural efficiency rather than improve it?

Reveal worked explanation

It may reduce stability, increase deflection or weaken the load path, so saving mass alone does not ensure the design still qualifies.

What to submit

Structural Efficiency & Preliminary Mass Budget

Complete the assigned worksheet/notebook or teacher-approved PDF. Keep data from the six actual 2.13 samples separate from fictional bridge examples and forward-looking mass estimates.

Required evidence

  • A worked load/mass comparison of the 120 N/100 g and 150 N/140 g hypothetical bridges, with units.
  • A table for all six 2.13 materials listing actual k, specimen mass and k/m where measured; document unavailable values explicitly.
  • At least two real specimen k/m calculations if the measurements exist, or a clear explanation if they do not.
  • A design screening comparing two or three bridge scenarios against span, width, load, deflection and mass limits.
  • An itemized proposed mass budget with all primary members, bracing, gussets, glue, M3 hardware and any printed fittings.
  • A 4–6 sentence design recommendation using at least two numerical comparisons and discussing one unresolved limitation.

Submission quality checklist

  • No fictional data are presented as actual student measurements or verified bridge load tests.
  • Each ratio is labeled N/g or N/(mm·g) correctly.
  • Missing test results are documented, not treated as k = 0.
  • The paint stick's different dimensions and composite orientation are considered.
  • Budget includes all connections and the 150 g finished mass limit.
  • Proposed bridge uses materials and joinery allowed by Unit 2.
  • The recommended concept passes every stated qualification criterion or clearly identifies required redesign.
