Forces, Loads & Structural Failure

Engineering question: What kinds of loads act on a wing, and how do those loads create different internal forces and failure modes?

Coming In

What You Already Know

You now know the wing must carry a 20 N design load while remaining light and stiff. Before choosing geometry, you need to understand what the load is actually doing to the structure.

Learning Targets

By the End of This Lesson

  • Distinguish external loads from internal structural forces.
  • Identify tension, compression, shear, bending, and torsion in a wing structure.
  • Predict likely structural failure modes and locations.
Learn the Concept

What You Need to Know

Use these ideas as your lesson notes. Focus on explaining the behavior, not memorizing vocabulary by itself.

External load vs. internal force

An external load is applied to the structure from outside. Inside the structure, members develop forces and stresses that transfer that load toward the support.

Tension and compression

Tension pulls material apart; compression pushes material together. When a spar bends downward, material on one side is stretched while material on the opposite side is compressed.

Shear and bending

Shear tends to slide adjacent sections past one another. Bending occurs when loads create curvature. A cantilever wing experiences both, with the most demanding region often near the fixed root.

Torsion

Torsion is twisting. Loads that do not pass through a section's effective center can twist a wing, which is one reason real wings use closed sections, skins, and multiple structural members.

Failure modes

Structures may fail by fracture, yielding, buckling, excessive deflection, joint separation, crushing, or local tearing. The weakest location—not the strongest member—often controls the final result.

Reading a loaded cantilever wing

A downward 20 N force applied near the tip bends the wing. Near the root, the spar must transfer the accumulated shear force and the largest bending moment into the fixture. The upper and lower regions of the spar experience opposite tension/compression effects. If the load is offset from the structural centerline, twisting can also appear.

Failure-Analysis Gallery Walk

Inspect Evidence Before Naming the Failure

  1. Rotate through teacher-provided photographs, failed samples, or structural examples.
  2. At each station, record what you can actually observe: bending, cracking, separation, twisting, crushing, or shape change.
  3. Use the evidence to identify the dominant load/internal force and the likely failure mode.
  4. After the gallery walk, transfer what you learned to the wing challenge by identifying three locations where failure may begin.
Lesson Evidence

Failure-analysis gallery record + wing transfer claim

  • Completed observations from the gallery-walk stations.
  • Failure-mode interpretations supported by visible evidence.
  • Three predicted wing failure locations with the likely mode.
  • A short claim explaining why the wing root is structurally critical.

Format: Use the compact gallery-walk recording sheet or teacher-approved notebook equivalent. The previous full worksheet is now a reference/reteaching resource rather than the primary activity.

Self-Check

Can You Explain It?

1. Where would you expect the largest bending demand in a cantilever wing?

Near the fixed wing root, because that section must resist the moment created by loads acting along the entire span.

2. What is the difference between bending and torsion?

Bending curves a member; torsion twists it about its longitudinal axis.
