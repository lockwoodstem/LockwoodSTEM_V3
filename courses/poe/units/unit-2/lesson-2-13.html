Coming In

What You Already Know

You created three wing concepts. Before choosing one, you will analyze what each design is likely to do under load.

  • Apply load-path and moment reasoning to proposed designs.

Learn the Concept

What You Need to Know

These notes are intended to be complete enough to learn the core idea directly from the page.

Analyze before scoring

A decision matrix is only as good as the evidence behind each number. Avoid scoring concepts first and inventing justifications afterward.

Load path quality

Check whether load can travel continuously from the 250 mm load location through spars/bracing/joints to the root.

Stiffness strategy

Identify what geometric feature is intended to limit deflection: section depth, dual spars, box geometry, truss depth, bracing, or another feature.

Mass estimate

Use approximate dimensions, material density, measured component mass, or simple comparison to estimate which concept is likely to approach the 75 g limit.

Failure prediction

Predict the first likely weak point and explain why: root joint, spar bending, compression buckling, rib tearing, adhesive separation, or another mode.

Evidence statement example

“Concept B should resist bending better than Concept A because its upper and lower spar caps are separated by a deeper section, but the additional ribs and second spar are likely to increase mass. The root joint remains the highest-risk area because it must transfer the full bending moment into the fixture.”

Apply It to Your Wing

Turn the Concept into Engineering Evidence

  1. Complete one analysis for each of your three concepts.
  2. Trace load path and mark likely tension/compression regions.
  3. Identify the stiffness strategy and predicted weak point.
  4. Estimate relative or numerical mass where possible.
  5. Reference at least one material-test result or previous lesson result in each concept analysis.
Lesson Deliverable

Wing concept analysis sheets

Turn in:

  • One analysis page/section for each concept.
  • Load path, stiffness strategy, material choice, mass estimate, and predicted failure.
  • At least one cited piece of prior unit evidence per concept.

Format: Engineering notebook, class template, CAD/design file, spreadsheet, photos, or another teacher-approved format as appropriate to the lesson.

Self-Check

Can You Explain It?

1. Why should a concept be analyzed before a decision matrix is filled out?

The scores should summarize engineering evidence. Without analysis, the matrix can become a disguised popularity vote.

2. What should a predicted failure point include besides a location?

A reason and failure mode, such as buckling from slender compression, bending at a low-stiffness spar, or separation at a weak joint.
