Materials Testing Lab

Engineering question: Which of our actual material strips resists bending most effectively—and how confident are we in the measurements?

Start LabBuild Test StationTesting StepsEnter Real DataGraph & AnalyzeWhat to Submit
Hands-on experimental engineering

Today we test real samples.

Use pre-cut strips of different materials, a digital force gauge and measurement tools to collect paired force–deflection readings.

These are actual experimental measurements—not the hypothetical material presets used in Lesson 2.11.

The mission

A fair comparison of materials

You will load strips across two supports, measure how far the midpoint deflects, then plot force against deflection. Use the graph's early straight-line slope to compare experimental bending stiffness.

01 · MeasureRecord actual width, thickness, support span, orientation and force-gauge units.
02 · TestApply increasing force carefully while measuring midspan deflection.
03 · ExplainGraph your paired measurements, compare slopes and identify experimental uncertainty.
Today's variables: Independent variable for comparison = material. Observed response = deflection under loading, or equivalently the force–deflection slope k = ΔF/Δδ. Controlled variables = support span, nominal specimen geometry, orientation, loading point, method and measurement technique. Always measure actual strip dimensions, even if pre-cut to identical nominal sizes.

01 / Station inventory

Equipment and responsibilities

You already have the pre-cut material samples and digital force gauges. Each group also needs a stable way to support and measure the strips.

Required equipment

  • At least two materials in strips of the same nominal size; label specimen IDs.
  • One digital force gauge with an appropriate compression range and safe contact tip.
  • Two equally high, fixed, rounded supports with measured and adjustable center-to-center spacing.
  • Metric ruler and calipers to measure span, width and thickness.
  • A fixed ruler or dial indicator measuring midpoint deflection; a fixed side camera with a metric reference scale is an acceptable observation aid.
  • Safety glasses, stable work surface and teacher-approved fixture or support arrangement.

Optional: digital scale for specimen mass, camera for evidence, a gauge stand to improve axial alignment and spreadsheets for analysis.

Assign a team of 3–4

Operator

Applies controlled, gradual load with gauge; communicates current reading.

Measurement lead

Observes center movement from a safe side position without touching the specimen.

Recorder / safety

Records paired values and watches for damage or test-limit exceedance.

Switch roles between specimens when time and equipment permit. No one holds the test strip by hand during loading.

02 / Set up the test

Three-point bending: two supports, one center load

Position the strip across two rounded supports and push straight downward at midspan using the digital force gauge. Measure the displacement of the strip itself, using a stationary reference attached to the fixture rather than the moving gauge.

Before loading

  1. Secure the fixture so the supports cannot slide or tip. Both supports must have the same height and a consistent contact shape.
  2. Choose a common measured span L that safely fits the shortest strips, with enough overhang at both ends to stay seated.
  3. Mark midpoint between the supports. Align a rounded gauge tip perpendicular to the strip over the mark.
  4. Fix a ruler/indicator or camera reference so deflection is read relative to the unloaded specimen, not relative to a moving hand.
  5. Check digital gauge capacity and set compression units to N if supported; otherwise convert readings before analysis.
  6. Under instructor supervision, gently contact the specimen, tare force appropriately and set displacement to zero at the same reference state without appreciable preload.

Measure each individual strip

Record strip overall length, width b, and thickness t at the center (mm). Also record grain/ply/print orientation, any knots, chips or defects and measured mass if a scale is available.

Cross-section I = bt³ / 12 (mm⁴)

Important: t is the thickness in the direction of bending. Incorrectly swapping b and t causes a large error because thickness is cubed.

Safety first: Eye protection is required. The teacher sets the allowable force, deflection and gauge capacity limits after a pilot test using the weakest sample. Do not deliberately snap, overload or launch fragments. Keep fingers, faces and measuring tools out of the loading path. Stop immediately at a crack, sudden force drop, slipping support, permanent kink or loss of alignment. Any failure-to-break experiment requires a separate teacher-approved shielded procedure.

04 / Student test procedure

Collect force and displacement together

The preferred approach is non-destructive, incremental loading. One student holds a small prescribed displacement while the recorder reads the force. Use an instructor-selected safe loading sequence based on gauge sensitivity and the weakest material.

Loading sequence

  1. Label and inspect the strip; measure its width/thickness and any visible defects before testing.
  2. Set the span: measure center-to-center support distance; keep it identical for each material.
  3. Seat and tare: position the strip and gauge, apply only a tiny seating load if authorized, then establish paired zero readings (0 N, 0 mm).
  4. Load slowly: increase deflection in small, teacher-specified steps. A 0.5 mm step is an illustrative example only, not a required or universally safe increment.
  5. At each step: one student holds the reading steady, another measures the vertical center displacement, and the recorder logs the simultaneous force and displacement.
  6. Stop at the approved limit: do not chase higher numbers after warning signs; record why the test stopped.
  7. Unload gradually: measure residual (permanent) displacement once the load returns near zero, if safe.
  8. Repeat with the next material, without changing the span, tip, measurement reference or loading method.

Data-quality rules

  • Record actual gauge force at each observed deflection; do not assume the force equals a target.
  • Record values with units: N and mm. If gauge reads lbf or kgf, convert to N before making a graph.
  • Use at least three nonzero paired readings in a safe approximately linear response region per specimen if equipment resolution permits; 5 or 6 is better.
  • If a specimen slips, a gauge is misaligned, or its curve becomes nonlinear, stop and annotate. Do not erase unexpected data silently.
  • Repeat with another specimen of the same material if available. Repeating a load cycle on the same strip is not an independent material replicate.
  • If a force gauge has a peak-hold mode, use live force readout for paired data. Peak hold can be used separately to document a maximum, but it does not pair a peak to a particular displacement automatically.

Instrument caution: Hand-held force gauges can introduce side forces and variable load rates. A fixed gauge stand and separate deflection indicator improve accuracy; without them, describe the result as an approximate comparison and report setup limitations.

07 / Analyze experimental evidence

Graph force against center deflection

Deflection belongs on the x-axis (mm) and force on the y-axis (N). The initial roughly linear relationship has a slope that represents the specimen's effective bending stiffness.

Your actual measured graph

Specimen ASpecimen BDashed = constrained-through-zero trend

The best-fit slope is calculated from your completed pairs using k = Σ(δF) / Σ(δ²), assuming readings were tared at (0, 0). Use only data in the visibly elastic, approximately linear portion. If the test has a preload or offset, correct the zero or use a different fit and document it.

From slope to an estimated modulus

k = ΔF / Δδ (N/mm)
I = bt³ / 12 (mm⁴)
Eestimated = kL³ / (48I) (N/mm²)

The model assumes a slender, simply supported, centrally loaded rectangular strip, small elastic deflection and a valid effective bending modulus. It is not a direct measurement of tensile strength, ultimate load, fracture toughness or guaranteed wood stiffness.

Example with invented practice data only: A classroom strip has span L = 120 mm, b = 10 mm, t = 3 mm, and measured early-region slope k = 2.0 N/mm. Then I = 22.5 mm⁴, so E ≈ 3,200 N/mm². This is an arithmetic demonstration, not today's measured class result.

Experimental accuracy: If deflection is close to the ruler's resolution, E estimates may be unreliable. Nonlinear response, shear deformation, support compliance, inaccurate thickness and imperfect center loading can also bias the slope. Comparing measured F/δ slopes may be more defensible than claiming a precise published material modulus.

How to interpret the comparison

1. Check geometrySame L and comparable b/t?
2. Check graphStraight near origin, or curved?
3. Compare kAt equal dimensions, larger N/mm means less bending per N.
4. Report caveatsRepeatability, grain, slippage, resolution and instrument support.

A strip with smaller deflection at a particular shared load is stiffer as tested. To make claims about the underlying material rather than only those specimens, account for actual cross-section dimensions, anisotropy and repeatability. Do not confuse stiffness with maximum strength or failure load.

08 / Lab report task

Make an evidence-based recommendation

Use two or more real materials, at least three usable nonzero readings per specimen and additional material replicates if available. Discuss the force–deflection graph, not just which strip “felt strongest.”

Required analysis questions

  1. Which physical strip had the largest initial force–deflection slope k? Provide the measured value and units.
  2. Which had the smaller deflection at a comparable applied load? If actual loads differed, use the graph trend or interpolation within your measured elastic region.
  3. How did measured width and thickness compare? Explain why even small differences in t can change bending stiffness.
  4. What observations indicated the strip remained elastic or began slipping, permanently deforming, cracking or otherwise behaving nonlinearly?
  5. What two limitations of your equipment or method could have changed the result? Propose one improvement.
  6. Given these results, which material would you investigate for a lightweight bridge member, and what additional strength, buckling or joint test would be needed before choosing it?

Real-world use of the results

Use actual specimen evidence to refine the material choices from 2.11. For the bridge capstone, selecting a material still requires consideration of mass, connection quality, compression buckling, grain direction and manufacturing rules.

The bridge's final performance requirements—including 400 mm clear span, 100 N center design load, ≤10 mm deflection and ≤150 g completed mass—apply to the final bridge, not directly to these small test strips.

Core insight: You are not declaring the “strongest material” from an elastic bending experiment. You are identifying which test specimen had a larger measured flexural stiffness and what more must be tested.

09 / Self-check

Try the calculation, then reveal the solution

Check A

Find the experimental stiffness

A sample requires 2 N at 0.5 mm, 4 N at 1 mm and 6 N at 1.5 mm. What is the force–deflection slope?

Reveal reasoning

The points follow F = kδ with k = 4 N/mm (e.g., 6 N / 1.5 mm). This describes specimen stiffness for this geometry and load range.

Check B

Why measure thickness?

Two nominally identical strips have the same width, but one is 2 mm thick and the other is 3 mm. Does that matter?

Reveal reasoning

Yes. For bending about the same axis, I = bt³/12. The 3 mm strip has (3/2)³ ≈ 3.38 times the second moment of area, so test deflection can differ greatly even for the same material E.

Check C

Gauge units

A digital gauge displays 1.0 kgf. What force should be entered in a graph labeled newtons?

Reveal reasoning

Approximately 9.81 N. Check the force gauge's selected units before transcription.

Check D

Nonlinearity

At the last loading step, force drops sharply while deflection increases. Should that point be included in the initial elastic slope fit?

Reveal reasoning

No. First document the event and stop according to safety limits. Fit only the clearly linear, elastic region; the sudden drop may indicate damage, slippage or another change that requires investigation.

Check E

Repeat trials

You load the same strip twice and obtain similar slopes. Have you tested two independent pieces of material?

Reveal reasoning

No. That is repeated loading of one specimen; a second physical strip is an independent sample and is better for estimating between-sample variability.

Check F

What can the test prove?

If plywood shows less deflection than balsa at 5 N, have you proven it will hold a larger ultimate load?

Reveal reasoning

No. The observation compares elastic bending response in the test configuration. Failure capacity requires separate approved tests and consideration of material dimensions and orientation.

Student submission

Exactly what to submit

Submit a POE 2.13 Materials Testing Lab Report (teacher-approved PDF, worksheet or notebook pages) with your real experimental measurements. Do not substitute model-generated values from 2.11.

Required report evidence

  • Objective, hypothesis and identification of independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  • One labeled diagram or photograph showing the actual support points, gauge contact location, span and deflection reference.
  • Material and specimen IDs, grain/orientation, dimensions (especially actual b and t), and force-gauge units.
  • At least three nonzero paired measured force–deflection readings for each of two materials, preferably more where safe.
  • A graph of force (N) versus center deflection (mm) with both materials identified and units labeled.
  • A clearly shown stiffness calculation (slope in N/mm) for each specimen; optional effective E calculation using the measured geometry.
  • Observations about return to original shape, residual set, slipping, damage or other notable behavior. Report "not observed" if no failure occurred.
  • A short conclusion comparing measured performance and explaining at least two limitations plus a specific improvement.

Submission quality check

  • Graphs and tables use real measurements—not assumed or fabricated data.
  • Sample span, orientation and loading method are consistent or deviations explained.
  • Initial slope is calculated from the linear elastic region only.
  • Repeat loading of one piece is not described as independent sample replication.
  • Stiffness results are not mislabeled ultimate strength or safe bridge capacity.
  • Exported CSV, printed data sheets or teacher-required evidence files are included.
