Learn the Concept

What You Need to Know

These notes are intended to be complete enough to learn the core idea directly from the page.

Stiffness vs. strength

Stiffness is resistance to deformation; strength is resistance to failure. A material can be strong but relatively flexible, or stiff but brittle.

Density

Density affects how much mass is added for a given volume. Low density is valuable only if the material still provides enough structural performance.

Toughness and brittleness

Tough materials absorb energy before fracture. Brittle materials may fail with little warning. Failure behavior matters during both testing and real service.

Manufacturability

A material that looks excellent on a property chart may be impractical if it cannot be cut accurately, joined reliably, or formed with available tools.

Joint compatibility

Adhesive bonding, fasteners, heat-set inserts, tabs, gussets, and printed connectors all impose different requirements on the materials they join.

Selection is a tradeoff

A very light wood strip may be easy to cut and glue but vulnerable to splitting. Thin plywood may be heavier but more dimensionally stable and useful for ribs or gussets. A 3D-printed fitting can create precise geometry but may be inefficient if used as the entire load-carrying wing.

Self-Check

Can You Explain It?

1. Why is density not enough to choose a wing material?

Low density helps reduce mass, but the material still needs sufficient stiffness, strength, reliability, and manufacturability.

2. Why should joint compatibility be part of material selection?

Loads must cross connections. A material that cannot be joined reliably can create a weak load path even if the material itself is strong.
