Coming In

What You Already Know

You now have structural principles and material-test evidence. This is the point where you deliberately generate solutions rather than immediately building your first idea.

Learning Targets

By the End of This Lesson

  • Generate multiple structurally distinct wing concepts.
  • Show a continuous load path in each concept.
  • Communicate geometry, materials, joints, and root attachment clearly enough for analysis.
Learn the Concept

What You Need to Know

These notes are intended to be complete enough to learn the core idea directly from the page.

Concepts must differ structurally

Changing a rib shape or cosmetic outline does not create a new engineering concept. Vary spar arrangement, bracing strategy, section type, load path, root connection, or structural architecture.

Think in subsystems

Separate the problem into root connection, primary spar system, secondary spar/bracing, ribs, tip region, and joints.

Design from the load path

Start with where the load enters and how it reaches the root. Add material because it has a job, not simply because space is available.

Sketch to communicate

A useful concept sketch includes multiple views or a clear 3D view, labels, approximate dimensions, material notes, and connection details.

Three valid directions

A single deep spar with ribs, a dual-spar rib structure, and a truss/box-style structure are genuinely different because they carry bending and shear in different ways. Three versions of the same single spar with different tip shapes are not.

Apply It to Your Wing

Turn the Concept into Engineering Evidence

  1. Generate at least three substantially different wing structures.
  2. Show the root connection and at least one primary load path on every concept.
  3. Label proposed materials, spar arrangement, ribs, bracing, and major joints.
  4. Add approximate dimensions and note one predicted strength and one predicted weakness for each concept.
Lesson Deliverable

Three wing concepts

Turn in:

  • Three clearly different annotated concepts.
  • Root connection and primary load path shown for each.
  • Materials, major dimensions, and joint strategy labeled.
  • One predicted advantage and one risk for each concept.

Format: Engineering notebook, class template, CAD/design file, spreadsheet, photos, or another teacher-approved format as appropriate to the lesson.

Self-Check

Can You Explain It?

1. What makes two concepts genuinely different?

They differ in meaningful structural architecture, load path, geometry, or connection strategy—not just appearance.

2. Why should you identify weaknesses during concept generation?

Recognizing risk early makes later analysis and selection more realistic and prevents preference-driven decisions.
